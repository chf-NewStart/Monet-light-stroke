# Monet Light & Strokes: Style System

Use this reference to translate “Monet feeling and strokes” into concrete image decisions. These principles were distilled across multiple user-provided photographs of river towns, sailboats, poplars, reflective water, and water-lily paintings. They describe a reusable visual system rather than one painting to copy.

## Core Impression

The scene should feel observed in a particular instant of weather and light. Objects remain legible, but atmosphere partly dissolves their edges. Color changes carry more information than line. Visible brushwork is purposeful and varies with the material being described.

Aim for:

- luminous air between viewer and subject;
- coarse, irregular broken color that optically mixes at viewing distance;
- a clear large-scale value structure beneath lively small marks;
- colored shadows and reflected light;
- selective detail, concentrated around the visual anchor;
- an unfinished-at-the-edge immediacy without losing source fidelity.

Avoid:

- uniform stippling over the entire canvas;
- tiny, evenly tiled mosaic marks or a digital brush-noise overlay;
- outlines around every object;
- sepia “old painting” filters;
- heavy palette-knife impasto on every surface;
- smooth gradients, glossy 3D rendering, or watercolor transparency;
- hyper-detailed facial pores or camera-like sharpness;
- copying the layout of any reference painting.

## Five-Rule Shorthand

Use these as the compact style test:

- **Broken color:** replace solid fields with neighboring dabs and strokes of varied pigment that mix optically. Build a golden reflection from yellow, ochre, coral, lilac, blue, and gray notes rather than one premixed gold fill.
- **Fleeting atmosphere:** organize the image around one specific instant of natural light, weather, moisture, and air. Paint how light alters the subject and surrounding color rather than describing local-object detail first.
- **Layered, selective impasto:** retain thin ground, scumble, broken middle paint, and partial revisions, then thicken only selected dabs and dashes at light-catching or structurally active passages. Do not coat the whole canvas in equal relief.
- **Serial observation:** when making a series, hold the subject and viewpoint steady while varying morning, overcast, sunset, twilight, season, or weather. Treat each image as a new observation, not a recolor or reused texture.
- **Chromatic edges:** define forms with shifts of hue, temperature, and value instead of black contours. Let reflected light enter silhouettes and allow secondary edges to soften or disappear into atmosphere.

## Light Keys

Choose one primary key and keep it coherent.

### Silver overcast

- Sky: pearl gray, pale blue, lavender, faint cream.
- Light: broad and diffuse with soft-edged shadows.
- Water: gray-blue and sage with cream glints.
- Best for river towns, harbors, quiet streets, and reflective scenes.

### Warm broken sun

- Light planes: butter cream, peach, dusty gold.
- Shadows: blue-violet, muted teal, mauve.
- Alternate warm and cool marks across architecture and skin.
- Best for towns, gardens, people outdoors, and late-afternoon scenes.

### Cool pond glow

- Field colors: blue-violet, smoky mauve, celadon, olive, pale turquoise.
- Accents: rose, coral, lilac, lemon, restrained white.
- Reflections may reverse ordinary depth and make the surface almost abstract.
- Best for water, floral subjects, dreamy portraits, and quiet profile images.

### Clear coastal air

- Sky: pale cyan and gray-blue with cream cloud masses.
- Water: blue-green, slate, and small white or yellow-green flashes.
- Boats and figures: economical dark anchors with warmer accent notes.
- Best for seascapes, beaches, and open horizons.

## Palette Logic

Build color through neighboring notes rather than one flat fill.

- Replace white with combinations of cream, pale peach, lilac, cool gray, and sky blue.
- Replace black with deep blue-green, violet-brown, charcoal blue, or dark olive.
- Place complementary temperatures side by side: peach beside blue-violet, rose beside green, gold beside cool gray-blue.
- Keep most saturation moderate. Reserve the clearest coral, cobalt, lemon, or emerald for a few accents.
- Let the same atmospheric colors recur in sky, ground, clothing, skin, and architecture so the scene feels unified.
- Preserve readable skin tone and identity; use warm/cool modulation without turning skin orange or gray.

## Perceptual Color Tuning

Translate the source's color relationships rather than copying literal sampled hues. Preserve the light direction, value hierarchy, recognizable skin and clothing families, and major warm-cool organization; allow local colors to shift within those constraints so the whole scene expresses one atmospheric instant.

- Create luminosity relationally. Place a few clean coral, gold, yellow-green, emerald, cobalt, or violet notes beside pearl gray, lavender gray, muted olive, and softened blue fields. The quieter field makes the accents appear brighter without requiring a globally brighter image.
- Lift chroma selectively on light-facing planes, reflected glints, thin trunks, foliage tips, flowers, and focal edges. Keep broad air, distant masses, and resting passages lower in chroma.
- Replace literal brown wood with related coral, peach, ochre, rose, violet-brown, or gold notes when the light supports it. Replace one generic green with neighboring yellow-green, olive, emerald, blue-green, and blue-violet passages.
- Let colored shadow intensify light: a warm trunk can carry cool blue-violet or green edge notes, while cool foliage can receive sparse peach or lemon light.
- Keep large twilight shadow masses ventilated by ambient color. Thread subdued blue, violet, teal, olive, rose, or reflected coral notes through dark plants and ground without raising the entire shadow value or outlining every form.
- Repeat important atmospheric hues across different materials at altered value and intensity. A warm trunk note may recur faintly in ground or reflection; a sky blue may enter foliage shadow.
- Keep reflections as tuned echoes rather than exact duplicates. Retain the reflected object's color family and vertical placement, but gray, break, interrupt, or warm-cool shift it according to the water.
- Judge the result by whether color makes the light more convincing and the painting more unified, not by whether each object matches a color-picker sample from the photograph.

Avoid global saturation, uniform exposure lifts, neon accents everywhere, crushed darks, pure white glare, one cinematic color grade, and hue changes that destroy identity or contradict the source light.

## Stroke Scale and Paint Surface

Make the painted construction visible without turning it into generic texture.

- Use three stroke scales: broad strokes for atmospheric and value masses, medium loaded strokes for most forms, and a few small accents at the focal point.
- Let broad and medium strokes dominate. Keep small detail marks to a minor share of the surface rather than tessellating the whole image.
- Favor fewer overlapping marks with irregular spacing, direction, length, thickness, and paint loading.
- Allow broad scumbled passages, dragged dry-brush edges, softened joins, and occasional underpainting or canvas grain to show between strokes.
- Load light-catching accents more thickly than shadow passages. Use impasto selectively on glints, flowers, foam, sunlit walls, or pale reflections.
- Keep calm passages beside active passages. Do not give every square inch the same density or sharpness.
- Make the dominant strokes readable at ordinary display size, not only when zoomed in.
- Avoid pixel-sized dabs, uniform pointillism, repeating leaf-shaped stamps, knitted texture, and evenly distributed palette-knife facets.

For water-lily and reflective-water scenes, begin with broad blue, violet, green, mauve, and cream masses; cross them with medium horizontal strokes; finish with only a few decisive flower, foam, or light dabs.

## Material-Specific Coarseness

Treat coarseness as a material description, not a canvas-wide effect. Vary at least four of these properties when moving between materials: stroke direction, stroke length, width, paint loading, opacity, edge quality, clustering, layer count, and quiet-space ratio.

Use the material-transfer test before accepting an image: imagine lifting a small representative patch from the sky and placing it unchanged on the ground, foliage, water, masonry, or a vertical plant. The transplant should look obviously wrong. If it still looks plausible, the image relies on a generic texture filter; reroute the marks by material.

- Let skies breathe through broad scumbles and merged transitions, with active broken notes clustered around cloud bands or light changes rather than equal dabs everywhere.
- Let ground accumulate irregular granular clusters, dragged skips, buried strokes, and perspective-compressed pauses rather than inheriting the sky's stroke rhythm.
- Let upright plants and trunks carry predominantly vertical structural marks, interrupted by lateral light, foliage, thorns, or broken edges.
- Let architecture retain a few planar horizontal and vertical anchors beneath softened atmosphere rather than dissolving into the same organic stipple as vegetation.
- Change texture across depth as well as material: foreground marks may be thicker and more varied; distance should use fewer, cooler, lower-contrast marks.

Avoid one repeated short-dab vocabulary, equal impasto across all surfaces, uniform horizontal hatching, and a procedural texture that remains convincing when moved between unrelated materials.

## Layered Oil Construction

Make the surface reveal accumulated decisions rather than one perfected rendering pass.

1. **Stained ground:** establish a thin muted undertone and allow irregular canvas weave or ground color to remain visible.
2. **Scumbled body:** lay broad, partly opaque color masses with dry or dragged edges. Let neighboring temperatures overlap without fully blending.
3. **Broken middle strokes:** add varied descriptive marks that change direction, width, length, and loading. Leave gaps and expose portions of earlier layers.
4. **Selective opaque accents:** place thicker light or dark marks only at crests, glints, faces, flowers, or structural anchors.
5. **Revision and burial:** partially cover some busy passages with fresh scumble, then re-enter them with only a few decisive marks. Retain imperfect joins, pentimenti, and interrupted edges.

Do not solve excessive texture by smoothing every stroke or converting the image into one family of elegant curves. Quiet an area by burying part of it beneath a broad imperfect veil while leaving fragments of earlier paint visible. Vary opacity, direction, temperature, and edge character between layers so the final surface has temporal depth.

Avoid a single-pass finish, evenly combed directional lines, globally polished transitions, uniform opacity, and a procedural embossed texture repeated across the canvas.

## Controlled Human Irregularity

Keep the scene faithful while allowing the painted surface to behave like a human process.

- Apply randomness at the mark level, not the subject level. Preserve composition, silhouettes, identities, anatomy, object counts, camera angle, light direction, and reflection anchors.
- Vary stroke angle within the material's directional family instead of forcing perfect parallelism. Water remains broadly horizontal, trunks broadly vertical, and masonry structurally planar.
- Vary pressure, paint loading, opacity, width, length, spacing, color temperature, and edge softness from mark to mark.
- Form uneven clusters and pauses. Let several active strokes collect near a focal passage, then leave adjacent areas quiet or thinly scumbled.
- Allow imperfect overlaps, partially covered strokes, dragged edges, dry-brush interruptions, and a few visible corrections.
- Introduce occasional restrained surprise notes—a cooler mark inside warm light or a warmer mark inside cool shadow—when they reinforce optical vibration.
- Permit some strokes to be transitional or atmospheric rather than literally descriptive. Do not require every mark to trace an object boundary.
- Avoid random noise, chaotic direction changes, evenly parameterized jitter, procedural scatter, or artificial “imperfection” applied uniformly everywhere.
- For a series, preserve the same ranges of stroke scale, palette, and material behavior while letting each image form a different mark pattern. Never reuse a visible texture map.

## Painting-First Abstraction

Preserve the source's factual skeleton while allowing its optical microstructure to be repainted.

- Lock the crop, subject count, silhouettes, identity, pose, key landmarks, major value bands, light direction, and essential reflection anchors.
- Treat individual ripples, sparkle patterns, leaf clusters, masonry texture, facial pores, minor seams, incidental clutter, and the exact pixel-level tonal map as negotiable.
- Begin from the scene as it appears through half-closed eyes. Reduce it to roughly five to nine dominant value-color masses before describing smaller forms.
- Reconstruct those masses with interlocking paint passages. Do not use the photograph as a texture map and do not trace every contrast boundary.
- Merge many repeated small forms into one irregular painted passage. Omit low-value information instead of translating every detail into a dab.
- Use lost-and-found edges. Keep only one to three focal edges relatively decisive; let most secondary boundaries soften, fragment, overlap, or disappear.
- Lower local contrast and mark density away from the focal anchor. Leave quiet scumbled areas beside active passages.
- Let color be optical rather than literal: invent restrained neighboring warm and cool notes that carry the same light and value relationships.
- At thumbnail size, recognizability should come from silhouette, placement, and large masses—not from an exact photographic tonal map.
- Default to moderate abstraction for people and architecture, and stronger abstraction for water, foliage, sky, reflections, and distant scenery.

Avoid a photograph with brush texture laid over it, edge-to-edge pixel tracing, HDR-like local contrast, preserving every wavelet or leaf, and uniform detail from foreground to distance.

## Stroke Routing

### Sky and air

- Use broad, lightly scumbled horizontal or diagonal strokes.
- Merge cloud edges into the surrounding air while retaining larger cloud masses.
- Keep the smallest detail near the horizon or visual anchor, not evenly everywhere.
- For an expressive dusk sky, allow an active coarse surface of overlapping gray-blue, lavender, rose, coral, peach, and muted orange marks. Let the color bands feel physically worked rather than smoothly graded or meteorologically precise.
- Preserve irregular ridges, buried marks, direction changes, and visible revisions when they make the sky read as paint. Do not equate refinement with smoothing or cleaning every transition.
- Balance activity through larger color masses and variable mark clusters; avoid a mechanically repeated texture map even when the overall surface is energetic.
- Preserve visible separation between selected neighboring sky notes so the eye completes part of the mixture at viewing distance. Let gray-blue, lavender, rose, coral, peach, and muted orange overlap at imperfect edges without fusing every transition into one seamless gradient.
- When an energetic sky feels too faceted, revise through partial burial, varied mark scale, softened interruptions, and overlapping scumble—not by blending the entire passage smooth. Retain some decisive raw-looking marks and ridges from the accepted version.

Calm mist, fog, or broad overcast may justify quieter and more merged passages, but even these should retain subtle changes in stroke, temperature, opacity, and edge character. Avoid treating smoothness as the default sign of refinement.

### Water and reflections

- Choose the water state before choosing its mark vocabulary.
- For calm reflective water, build the surface from short horizontal or slightly diagonal broken strokes over broad connected color fields.
- Reflect major objects as loose vertical bands of their dominant colors.
- Interrupt those bands with horizontal ripples so they never read as mirrored vector shapes.
- Add a few light glints without covering the entire surface in white.

For choppy open sea:

- Begin with a thin muted gray-green, blue-gray, olive, or source-derived reflected ground. Cross it with broad semi-opaque scumbles so the sea has a continuous body while fragments of ground and canvas remain visible.
- Carry movement with medium-to-long curved strokes, shallow hooks, loose S-curves, and dragged diagonal sweeps that travel across several implied ripples. Let gestures overlap and change tempo.
- Shape a wave as a rising dark-green or blue-gray body, a turning lighter plane, and a sparing broken crest. Do not assemble it from separate rectangular patches.
- Apply foam as thin dragged cream, pearl, pale gray, or warm-white ribbons, dry-brushed curls, and interrupted flicks. Keep it uneven and partially absorbed into the water color.
- Use fewer, longer gestures in the foreground and compress marks toward distance. Alternate active crests with broad quiet water passages.
- Preserve several layers simultaneously: subdued undercolor, broad scraped or scumbled bodies, irregular broken wave strokes, and sparse opaque foam. Let later strokes partly bury earlier ones instead of aligning every mark into one smooth flow.
- Permit subdued muddy-neutral mixtures within the water body while keeping foam and reflected light clean. The field should feel optically mixed, not brightly tiled.
- Preserve the source's wave bands and light direction, but invent the exact curvature, overlap, and interruption of individual painted gestures.

Avoid chunky palette-knife blocks, checkerboard color slabs, polygonal tessellation, uniformly short horizontal dashes, thick white frosting on every crest, equal stroke density across the entire water surface, and a single smooth repeating wave pattern that looks digitally combed.

### Ground, sand, and gravel

- Treat the surface as an approximate broken value field before describing individual particles, footprints, or stones.
- Layer broad umber, gray-violet, blue-gray, olive, muted cream, and source-light colors; cross them with irregular dabs, short dragged marks, dry-brush skips, and partially buried strokes.
- Let warm and cool glints scatter unevenly near light sources while large shadow zones remain chromatic and unresolved.
- Preserve the ground plane, slope, path, and major shadow placement, but allow local texture to be invented and physically worked.
- Favor a coarse granular rhythm with clusters, gaps, overlaps, and changing paint load. It should read as painted sand or gravel from a distance, not as precisely rendered pebbles.

Avoid tracing every stone, evenly stamped granules, sharp photographic footprints, perfectly clean perspective texture, smooth digital fill, and over-refining an accepted rough passage into precise illustration.

### Foliage and flowers

- Use clustered dabs, hooks, short commas, and uneven color patches.
- Describe tree mass before individual leaves.
- For poplars, combine tall vertical trunks with irregular blue-green and yellow-green leaf clusters.
- Let floral accents sit on top of the color field as a handful of warmer, sharper marks.

### Cacti and succulent forms

- Lock each cactus silhouette, height, placement, rib count impression, and overlap before painting surface variation. Do not invent arms, flowers, or additional plants.
- Build columnar volume with elongated vertical and slightly curving rib-following strokes in deep olive, blue-green, charcoal blue, violet-green, and muted reflected sky color.
- Break selected outer edges with sparse warm or cool rim-light notes; keep the darkest body chromatic rather than black.
- Suggest thorns with only a few interrupted fine touches near focal or rim-lit areas. Do not cover the plant with evenly spaced dots.
- Use selective thicker paint on a few lit ribs and edge accents while leaving shadow ribs thinner, quieter, and partly scumbled.

Avoid rendering cacti with the sky's broad scumbles, the ground's granular dabs, uniformly repeated scales, hard black cutout silhouettes, or photographic spine-by-spine detail.

### Architecture

- Establish walls with blocky horizontal and vertical strokes.
- Let sunlit facades fragment into cream, peach, pale yellow, lilac, and reflected green-blue.
- Suggest windows and rooflines with a few dark marks; do not outline every edge.
- Reduce contrast and precision as buildings recede.

### People and portraits

- Preserve face, pose, expression, body proportions, outfit colors, accessories, and silhouette.
- Model faces with connected warm and cool planes, then add only the essential eyes, brows, mouth, and hair accents.
- Use slightly smaller marks around the face and hands, broader strokes in clothing and background.
- Keep the entire visible body when it is present in the source. Do not improve likeness by zooming in.

### Boats, props, and small figures

- Use a few concise dark marks to anchor scale and direction.
- Preserve recognizable props and their placement.
- Avoid inventing extra boats, people, flowers, or accessories merely to make the scene “more painterly.”

## Depth and Edge Hierarchy

- Foreground: thicker, more varied strokes; strongest dark-light accents; warm/cool vibration.
- Middle ground: recognizable shapes with softened boundaries.
- Distance: cooler, lighter, lower-contrast masses with fewer marks.
- Focal edge: one or two areas may be relatively crisp.
- Peripheral edges: dissolve into adjacent atmosphere while keeping the outer composition intact.

## Motif Recipes

### River town

Use a pale atmospheric sky, a compressed band of softened cream-gray buildings, one strong vertical tree or tower, tiny dark figure or boat accents, and broad reflective water. Echo sunlit facades in broken cream and lavender glints below.

### Seascape with sails

Keep the horizon stable but not ruler-sharp. Establish a continuous muted blue-green or gray-green sea body, then shape its movement with irregular arcing, hooked, and dragged strokes. Reserve broken pale ribbons for selected foam crests. Let sails be warm triangular notes against cool air; keep hulls and distant boats minimal.

### Poplar rhythm

Treat tall trunks as repeated warm verticals crossing cooler green-blue foliage. Vary spacing and thickness. If water is present, extend broken vertical reflections downward and disrupt them with ripples.

### Pond or water-lily field

Allow the surface to fill most or all of the composition. Layer blue, violet, green, mauve, and pale reflected light without a conventional horizon. Place lily pads as flattened elliptical patches and blossoms as sparse rose, lilac, cream, or yellow accents.

### Full-body portrait outdoors

Keep the full silhouette, hands, legs, shoes, clothing, and surrounding context. Use the figure as the clearest value anchor, but let environmental light enter the skin and clothing. Preserve enough space around the body that the result still reads as a scene rather than a cropped portrait.

## Prompt Scaffold

Adapt this scaffold rather than copying it verbatim:

> Preserve [factual source invariants] and the complete original framing, including [full-body or prop details]. Keep [aspect ratio/camera angle]. Do not preserve photographic microtexture or trace the exact tonal map. Repaint the scene as an original luminous French Impressionist oil painting organized by [light key]. First reduce the scene to five to nine connected value-color masses, then rebuild selected forms through adjacent broken-color strokes and chromatic shadows. Render [material 1] with [stroke behavior], [material 2] with [stroke behavior], and distant forms with cooler low-contrast marks. Merge or omit minor repeated details, retain only a few focal edges, and let secondary edges dissolve. Allow controlled hand-made variation in stroke angle, length, pressure, spacing, overlap, and temperature while keeping all scene facts fixed. Keep the face and body recognizable while simplifying them into painterly planes. No crop, paint-over-photo filter, hard outlines, smooth digital gradients, flat black shadows, plastic skin, copied composition, signature, text, watermark, or unrequested frame.

For original generation, replace preservation language with a clear scene specification while retaining the same light, palette, stroke, depth, and exclusion sections.

## Retry Language

When the first output fails, diagnose the exact failure and issue a targeted edit:

- **Crop failure:** “Zoom out and restore the full body from head through both shoes, with margin around the figure; keep all other content unchanged.”
- **Identity drift:** “Restore the source facial structure, hairstyle, expression, skin tone, and body proportions; retain the painterly rendering.”
- **Generic texture:** “Vary stroke direction by material: broad sky scumbles, broken horizontal water marks, clustered foliage dabs, and blocky architectural strokes.”
- **Coarse but uniform:** “Run the material-transfer test and replace the canvas-wide short-dab texture. Use broad irregular sky scumbles, vertical rib-following cactus strokes, simplified planar village marks, and clustered dragged ground strokes, with different loading, opacity, edge quality, and quiet-space ratio in each passage.”
- **Blocky sea:** “Replace rectangular color slabs with a continuous muted water body crossed by medium-to-long arcing and hooked wave gestures. Drag a few thin broken cream foam ribbons across selected crests, leave quiet scumbled passages, and keep the source's large wave bands fixed.”
- **Too smooth or AI-like:** “Restore visible paint chronology: expose fragments of muted ground, cross them with broad imperfect scumbles, retain irregular broken middle strokes, add only a few opaque accents, and partially bury selected busy passages. Do not regularize the water into one smooth repeating curve pattern.”
- **Over-blended sky:** “Restore distinct neighboring gray-blue, lavender, rose, coral, peach, and muted-orange marks across the sky. Let them overlap and partially bury one another while preserving visible color separation and irregular ridges; do not convert the atmospheric transition into a seamless gradient or flat uniform surface.”
- **Heavy dead foreground shadow:** “Keep the source's dark value structure, but ventilate cactus, foliage, and ground shadows with restrained blue, violet, teal, olive, reflected coral, and warm-gray notes. Preserve darkness through value relationships rather than near-black fill.”
- **Too mechanical:** “Break the repeated pattern with uneven clusters and pauses, varied pressure and loading, imperfect overlaps, dry-brush interruptions, and a few restrained warm/cool surprise notes; keep every scene-level fact unchanged.”
- **Muddy color:** “Separate warm light from blue-violet and green shadows using adjacent clean notes; reduce brown and black.”
- **Dull or overly literal color:** “Preserve the source's value hierarchy, light direction, and recognizable color families, but tune local hues into neighboring warm and cool notes. Place a few clean light-catching accents beside quieter pearl, lavender-gray, olive, and blue-gray fields so luminosity comes from contrast rather than a global brightness increase.”
- **Globally bright or oversaturated:** “Lower chroma and value across the broad atmospheric field while retaining a few clear coral, gold, yellow-green, emerald, cobalt, or violet accents at light-facing and focal passages. Restore chromatic shadow and quiet gray intervals; do not dim every color equally.”
- **Too photographic:** “Discard the source's optical microtexture while preserving its factual skeleton. Reduce the scene to five to nine large color-value masses, merge repeated small details into irregular painted passages, remove most secondary edges and local contrast, and rebuild the image in visible paint rather than tracing pixels.”
- **Too precise or cleaned up:** “Keep the factual silhouette and large masses, but collapse secondary sky, sand, ground, foliage, and distant architecture into visibly worked color fields. Restore coarse overlaps, buried marks, broken edges, approximate texture, and irregular paint loading; do not polish them into exact illustration.”
- **Too abstract:** “Restore the subject silhouette, landmarks, facial structure, and prop placement; keep abstraction inside the painted surfaces, not the factual composition.”
